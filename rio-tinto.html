The business problem

Find the signal across hundreds of company newsrooms.

Mining intelligence starts with scattered announcements. Our capstone with Rio Tinto explored how to convert those releases into consistent survey records, helping analysts track exploration activity and focus their research.

Working with our partners, we narrowed roughly 20 overlapping event categories to geophysics and geochemistry surveys. Understanding the exploration roadmap, from prospecting and surveys to discovery and monitoring, helped us choose three useful fields.

Survey type
Geophysics, geochemistry, both, or neither.
Completion status
Planned, in progress, ended, or results released.
Survey dates
Start and end dates, when supported by the release.

The data engineering

Connect collection, classification, and extraction.

The project combined two data streams: a historical corpus for model development and newly collected releases for the mining-intelligence workflow.

Historical corpus

Build the training foundation.

Approximately 33,000 unlabeled excerpts, each containing the first two paragraphs of a press release. These concise passages typically carried the key announcement.

Output: GPT-assisted training labels

Web collection

Gather new company announcements.

A CSV of 1,090 company news-directory URLs led to 9,872 collected releases from 638 companies. Releases averaged roughly 900 words.

Output: text, title, and publication date where available

The modelling challenge

Learn from a corpus that arrived without labels.

We first explored topic modelling and zero-shot classification. After discussing the results with our academic advisor, Professor Zhu, we moved to supervised models, using GPT-3.5 Turbo to generate training annotations and human review to assess their quality.

  1. Start with 5,000 releases. Use an initial prompt to assign survey-type and status labels.
  2. Build a 150-example benchmark. Sample across predicted class combinations and manually annotate the examples with Rio Tinto partners.
  3. Refine prompts, then label the corpus. Compare prompt outputs with the reviewed examples before annotating roughly 33,000 historical excerpts.
  4. Train task-specific models. Compare survey-type and status classifiers, then use question answering for survey dates.
Evaluation context

Survey type was evaluated on 150 annotated examples across four classes: Geophysics Survey, Geochemistry Survey, Both, and Others. The 45 examples in the Others class did not describe geophysics or geochemistry surveys, so they did not require completion-status labels. Completion status was therefore evaluated on the remaining 105 labeled survey examples across three classes: Planned, In-progress, and Ended.

Zero-shot & topic modelling
We tried one- and two-step Contriever classification, Sentence Transformer similarity, LDA, and BERTopic. Relevance thresholds were difficult to set; BERTopic produced interpretable topics, but forcing fewer topics reduced usefulness.
Supervised classifiers
We compared DistilBERT, fastText, DistilRoBERTa, and a bidirectional LSTM using DistilBERT representations. The LSTM and pooling experiments did not deliver the improvement we sought. fastText provided a lightweight word/subword baseline.
Training & imbalance
The workflow covered preprocessing, tokenization, initialization, and iterative training. We downsampled majority classes; the documented DistilBERT configuration used 20 epochs and a batch size of 32. Evaluation included accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and confusion matrices.
Adding survey context
For DistilRoBERTa, the survey-type classifier read the first two paragraphs. The status classifier also received survey type as an input, providing context for the Planned, In-progress, and Ended categories.

Flan-T5 · Question-answering extraction

Extract dates while preserving their meaning.

80.54%

Reported date-extraction accuracy

Flan-T5 achieved 80.54% reported accuracy for survey-date extraction. The workflow combined survey-specific questions with answer scoring and date parsing.

Development experiments highlighted three issues that shaped how date answers should be interpreted:

A year became a false exact date
A parser could turn “2013” into “2013-06-14” by filling missing fields with the current month and day. A correct year then became an unsupported full date.
Valid partial dates were lost
Expressions such as “mid-July” did not always standardize cleanly. Rejecting every unparseable answer removed some useful information.
The correct date landed in the wrong field
A completion date assigned to “start” instead of “end” counted as a mismatch. Survey status and date-field semantics needed to be considered together.

Score-only filtering was insufficient; parsing alone also discarded valid answers. The final approach combined both. Future work should preserve the precision actually present in the source.

Business value & lessons

Make exploration news easier to investigate.

The project brought web collection, LLM-assisted annotation, and supervised NLP into one workflow. Its practical value was turning scattered announcements into structured survey information that could support analysts’ research and investment discussions.

Scope the problem with the people using it.

Partner discussions turned a broad mining-event tracker into a concrete task: identify surveys, their status, and their dates.

Review the labels before trusting the score.

LLM annotations made training feasible, while the development benchmark exposed imbalance and error propagation. Reporting test-set sizes and task-specific accuracy makes the results easier to interpret; class-level checks remain important for less common survey events.

Keep the evidence and its uncertainty.

False positives, missing dates, and partial dates affect how useful a record is to an analyst. Next steps include broader date-format support and status-aware extraction, potentially with separate date models for different release types.
